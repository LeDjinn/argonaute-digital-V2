A working demo tells a reviewer what you built. It does not necessarily explain why that design fits the problem. For a developer portfolio, I would add one short architecture decision record before adding another framework badge. The interesting evidence is not that you chose PostgreSQL, Next.js or a queue. It is that you can describe a credible alternative and explain the cost of declining it.

This is a proposed skills exercise, not a claim about hiring demand or a guarantee of interviews. Use a project you own, publish only non-sensitive context, and label hypothetical requirements as hypothetical.

## Choose a decision with something to lose

AWS describes an architecture decision record, or ADR, as a record of a significant choice, its context and its consequences.[1] Microsoft recommends limiting records to choices affecting structure, important quality attributes or decisions that are difficult to reverse.[3] My recommendation: skip the naming convention debate and pick a boundary that changes operational responsibility.

Consider a hypothetical reporting application with a React interface, a Next.js API and PostgreSQL. The decision is whether report generation belongs inside the request or behind a durable job boundary. Do not begin with “queues scale better.” Begin with the requirements you actually have: acceptable waiting time, cancellation expectations, retry behavior and who will operate the worker.

Write unknowns explicitly. If report duration has not been measured, say so. A number invented to justify your preferred architecture is worse than an unresolved question.

## Give the rejected option a fair hearing

For this exercise, compare three designs: synchronous generation, an asynchronous job backed by the existing database, and a managed queue with a separate worker. Treat these as candidates to investigate, not interchangeable implementations or universal recommendations.

Use the same questions for each option. What happens after the API accepts work? Who retries a failed attempt? Where does the customer see progress? What must be deployed, monitored and recovered? Which failures require manual intervention?

I would reject a comparison that gives the preferred option concrete benefits and its competitors only slogans. Microsoft explicitly includes ruled-out alternatives, tradeoffs and decision confidence in its ADR guidance.[3] Make the strongest case for the simpler design, then name the requirement it cannot satisfy under your stated assumptions.

For example, I might propose a job boundary because this hypothetical product requires accepted reports to remain recoverable after the initiating request ends. That is a design requirement, not proof that a particular queue configuration meets it. The record should point to the implementation and recovery checks that would establish that behavior.

## Separate evidence from the decision

Keep three labels visible: observed, assumed and chosen. “A representative export exceeded our request budget” needs a reproducible measurement. “Customers will tolerate delayed delivery” is an assumption until investigated. “We will expose a job-status endpoint” is a proposed design choice.

Google Cloud's ADR outline includes context, requirements, key options and reasons for the accepted choice.[2] I would attach a small evidence bundle: a synthetic workload, commands to run it, the relevant configuration and an honest summary of what remains untested. Do not publish production records or credentials to make the artifact look realistic.

Connect the choice to its failure semantics. [Background Jobs: Make Retries Safe Before Making Them Fast](/en/blog/retry-safe-background-jobs) is a useful companion when the proposal introduces retries. For database rollout consequences, use [PostgreSQL Migrations Without Release Traps](/en/blog/postgresql-migrations-without-release-traps) rather than treating schema changes as an implementation footnote.

## State what would change your mind

My preferred record ends with an explicit cost and a reconsideration trigger. For the proposed job design, the cost might be an additional worker, status handling and recovery procedures. A trigger might be evidence that the operational burden exceeds the value of surviving request termination for this product.

Keep that trigger separate from permission to rewrite history. AWS treats accepted records as immutable and uses a new record to supersede an old decision.[1] I would preserve the original assumptions, then link a replacement explaining what changed. A portfolio revision is more useful when a reviewer can follow the correction.

## Make the artifact reviewable

Deliver one concise record, a diagram of the affected boundary and links to the checks supporting the decision. Ask a reviewer to challenge the weakest assumption, not merely approve the chosen technology. Keep detailed experiments outside the record so the decision remains easy to find.

In my view, this demonstrates a valuable engineering habit: choosing under constraints without pretending the rejected alternatives were foolish.
